The hour flew. When she fumbled a question about budget forecasting, he heard himself say, "Nerves, totally understandable," and move on.

Bernadette Pugh interviewed at two. She was heavyset, with a pockmarked complexion and thick glasses she kept pushing up her nose. Martin caught himself checking his watch at minute twelve. When she paused over the same budget question, he wrote "hesitant" in the margin.

His co-panellist, Yolanda Ferris, had been quiet all day. Afterwards, she slid her scoresheet across the desk.

"I used the rubric," she said. "Same five questions, scored before I looked up. Bernadette answered the forecasting question better. She just took longer to start. Sienna never actually answered it."

Martin looked down at his own notes. Nerves, totally understandable. Hesitant.

He read them again. Then he picked up the phone and invited both women back for a second round: a written case exercise, names removed, marked by Yolanda and someone from finance who had never seen either face.

Bernadette's answer was sharper and more detailed, and it caught an error in the sample data that the finance team had missed for a year.

She started in October. Martin kept the two notes, nerves and hesitant, pinned above his desk.

The storm that took out a section of the boundary fence between Walt Gruber's farm and the Ribeiro house on Fell Lane also took out most of the apple tree that had marked the line for forty years. Walt was a retired dairy farmer who had voted for every council candidate promising lower rates and fewer wind turbines. Ana Ribeiro taught history at the sixth-form college and had spent the last election arguing that the turbines were the only sensible answer for the valley's future.

The council sent a letter saying the repair was the responsibility of both properties. Walt wrote back that he would pay half if Ana agreed to put up a sign saying the turbines were a mistake. Ana wrote back that she would pay half if Walt agreed the sign would hang on his side of the fence.

Neither of them expected the other to show up on Saturday morning, but both did, each with a pair of gloves and a tool bag. Walt brought fence posts and a hammer that had been his father's. Ana brought wire, a flask of tea, and a box of flapjacks from the bakery on the high street.

They worked in silence for an hour. Then Walt grumbled that the posts were not straight. Ana said that was because the ground was soft from the rain. Walt said the ground had been soft since 1987, and Ana laughed, which surprised them both.

By noon they were arguing about the wind turbines again, but more quietly, and with the occasional concession. Walt admitted the new road had improved the valley's bus service. Ana admitted the turbines had been louder than the brochures promised. They ate flapjacks standing by the finished fence, and when the last wire was tight, Walt shook her hand without saying anything at all.
