Elias closed eyes that saw nothing and drew the bow across the strings. The first note was a deep, resonant hum that vibrated through the floorboards. To his left, Mrs. Gable, the violinist, leaned forward, her pen hovering over her clipboard. To his right, Mr. Vance, the percussionist, stopped tapping his foot.

Elias did not see their expressions, but he heard the shift in the room's acoustics. He heard the soft intake of breath as the melody climbed, weaving through the air like smoke. His fingers moved with practiced precision, finding the high notes by memory and muscle rather than sight. He played the sorrow of a winter storm, then the sudden, sharp joy of a breaking dawn. The silence that followed the final note was heavier than the music itself.

"Again," Halloway whispered, breaking the spell.

Elias smiled, though no one could see it. He reset his bow.

"Wait," Mrs. Gable interrupted, her voice trembling slightly. "No need. That was enough."

Mr. Vance nodded vigorously. "He didn't just play the notes. He played the room."

Halloway stood up, the chair scraping loudly against the floor. "Elias Thorne," he said, his tone softer now. "You have the chair. Welcome to the orchestra."

Elias lowered his bow, a warmth spreading through his chest that had nothing to do with the drafty room. He had not seen them, but he knew they were listening.

Table nine at Clara and Dev's wedding was the table organisers use for people who might otherwise have to be introduced to everyone else. "So what do you do?" Helen asked Jonas, though she'd already been told.

The Delgado family reunion took over three picnic shelters at Lakeview Park, and by noon Rosa Delgado had been asked the question nine times.

Her cousin Héctor asked it over the grill, flipping a burger: "So, Rosa, still no one special?" Her Tía Carmen asked it while pinning a name tag to Rosa's blouse, as though the tag might one day need room for a second surname. Her brother's mother-in-law, a woman named Dolores whom Rosa had met twice, asked it with real sympathy, which was worse.

Rosa was fifty-two. She ran the structural engineering department for the county, had restored a 1931 bungalow almost entirely with her own hands, and had swum the Strait of Juan de Fuca for charity. None of this came up.

"Still no one special," she told Dolores. "Just me and the house."

"Well," Dolores said, patting her hand. "There's still time."

At three, Abuela Inés, ninety-one and enthroned in a lawn chair, beckoned her close. Rosa braced herself.

"Mija," said Inés. "You never married."

"No, Abuela."

"Good." The old woman's eyes were sharp. "I married at seventeen. I loved your abuelo. But I always wondered what I would have built."

Rosa laughed, startled, and something in her chest loosened.

Later, her twelve-year-old niece, Valeria, found her down by the lake and sat beside her on the dock. Rosa waited for question number fourteen.

"Tía," Valeria said, "how did you swim across a whole strait?"

Rosa took off her sandals and put her feet in the water.

"Badly, at first," she said. "Let me tell you."
