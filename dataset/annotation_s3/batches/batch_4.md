Wren asked if she could look. Pauline, who was busy, said yes, mostly to get rid of her.

Twenty minutes later, Mr. Gilchrist, the retired organist who still came on Thursdays to dust the pipes out of grief, found the child inside the organ case with a torch in her teeth.

"Come out of there at once," he said. "That is a historic instrument."

"Your trackers have come off," Wren said around the torch. "Three of them. And there's a mouse nest in the swell box. That's why the low C sticks."

Gilchrist opened his mouth and shut it again. He had suspected mice for years.

Wren's mother, Fiona, came looking for her an hour later and found the vicar, the organist and her daughter crouched together in the organ loft while Wren explained the coupler mechanism with a diagram she'd drawn on the back of a hymn sheet.

"She does this," Fiona apologised. "Last month it was our boiler."

By Sunday, Gilchrist and Wren had reattached the trackers with button thread and patience. When he played the opening chords of the first hymn, the whole congregation turned around in their pews.

Wren, in the front row, wasn't listening to the music. She was listening for the low C. It didn't stick.

Dr. Ayesha Karimi had been staring at the same petri dish since ten, and at midnight she finally admitted she was not alone. The floor buffer in the corridor stopped. Mr. Anselm Duarte, who had cleaned the microbiology lab for eleven years, leaned in the doorway with his mop.

"You still here, doctor? The dishes will not grow faster if you watch them."

"They might," Ayesha said, rubbing her eyes. "Come look at this."

He crossed the room, wiping his hands on his apron. In the dish, a ring of clear space surrounded a tiny fuzzy fleck of mould, and the bacteria beyond it were sagging, dying.

"The mould is killing them," Anselm said slowly.

"That's what I think. But I'd assumed my sample was contaminated and I almost threw it out. Which dish is this?"

"Number fourteen, in the corner." He frowned. "I moved that one to the back shelf last week. It was in the way. I'm sorry. It had a crack in the lid."

Ayesha stared at him. "A crack. So the spores got in from the corridor."

"From the air vent, maybe. Near the old sink."

She grabbed a swab. "Anselm, where exactly is that vent?"

He led her down the hall to the crumbling grille above the sink, and together they scraped a sample into a fresh tube. At two in the morning, Ayesha wrote his name in the lab notebook beside hers, in capital letters.

"Careful," he said, smiling. "They'll make me famous."

=== STORY R176 ===
By four in the afternoon the rain on Carver Street was coming sideways, and the drain outside Quinn's Garage had given up.

Bernadette Quinn was stacking sandbags across her bay door when she saw water creeping under the glass front of Shear Luca, the salon opposite. Luca Ferraro was inside with a mop and a client, an elderly woman named Mrs. Abernathy, who still had foils in her hair.

Bernadette splashed across the road, knocked, and shouted through the door. "You've got about ten minutes before that's ankle deep!"

Luca opened it. "I have twenty minutes left on her colour. I can't move her."

"Then I'll move the water." Bernadette ran back for the spare sandbags and her wet-vac, and for the next half hour she built a low wall across the salon's doorway while Luca rinsed Mrs. Abernathy at the basin and pretended everything was normal.

At six, the power went out on the whole street. Bernadette's garage had a generator.

Luca carried his hairdryer and a folding chair across the road. Mrs. Abernathy followed under an umbrella, foils glinting. In the back of the garage, between a stripped-down Ford Fiesta and a rack of tyres, Luca finished her blow-dry by the light of an inspection lamp.

"Best I've ever had," Mrs. Abernathy announced.

When she had gone, Luca looked at Bernadette's hair, which was soaked and stuck flat with engine grease.

"Sit," he said, pointing at the chair.

"I'm fine."

"You built me a wall. Sit."

She sat, and he got out his scissors.
